## ICP 1 — Derek (Primary · The Experienced Lifter · 27–40)

**Training:** 4–5x/week | **Supplement spend:** $100–200/month | **Currently on:** protein + pre-workout, has used creatine before

Derek has been lifting for 5+ years. He's taken creatine before — probably a generic monohydrate, maybe BPN's, maybe something from Costco. He responds to the premium sourcing and purity angle when it's explained. He's already spending on protein and pre-workout — supplement quality differences register with him.

His silent concern: *Is any creatine actually meaningfully better than the $15 bag?* He's open to being convinced, but he needs a specific mechanism — not a vibe.

**How to win him:** Pürest Creatine® purity + acetic acid = two real mechanisms in one formula. "This isn't just creatine monohydrate" is credible to him IF you explain what the second ingredient actually does. Don't just say it's better. Show him the mechanism. The BJJ round 4 testimonial in the VOC is the most differentiated social proof in the dataset — lead with it.

**Creative instinct:** Outcome specificity. "Up 15 lbs on my bench in 3 weeks. Nothing else changed." Let a real review do the work. Derek trusts specificity, not aspiration.

## ICP 2 — Tyler (Secondary · The Endurance Athlete · 25–42)

**Sport:** Running, cycling, CrossFit, BJJ, OCR | **Current stack:** Electrolytes, beta-alanine, maybe beetroot powder

Tyler has written off creatine as a bodybuilder supplement. He's not interested in bulking or "getting jacked." He wants to go longer, recover faster between hard efforts, and sustain output in the back half of his events. His mental model of creatine: ATP, explosiveness, short-duration power. Not for him.

**How to win him:** Acetic acid promotes glycogen replenishment in skeletal muscle — that's your endurance fuel between efforts. Atlas isn't just an ATP booster — it's a full-arc performance supplement that addresses what happens after your first 20 minutes. Category reframe first, product second. "You've been told creatine is for lifters. Here's what it actually does when you're running mile 8."

**Creative instinct:** Address the belief directly before the product pitch. Sport-specific creators (BJJ, CrossFit, running) are mandatory — not a fitness influencer with a 2% bodyfat physique.

## ICP 3 — Ashley 🟢 Green Field (Underserved · The Creatine-Curious Woman · 24–38)

**Training:** Strength training 3–4x/week | **Following:** Women's lifting TikTok, "girls who lift"

Completely unaddressed in current Atlas creative. Zero female UGC. Zero myth-busting content. Zero female-targeted paid ads have ever existed for Atlas. Ashley has seen creatine content. She knows she probably should take it. She's been held back by three myths no brand has properly debunked:

- Will it cause hair loss?
- Will I get bloated and puffy?
- Will it make me bulky?

None are scientifically valid. Not one competitor has addressed them in paid creative.

**How to win her:** Lead with myth debunk (hair loss, bloating). Female creator is mandatory — ideally someone who's been lifting 2–3 years, not a competitive bodybuilder. Close on 6-week outcome: "Strength is up. Zero bloat. My hair is fine." Atlas's unflavored, clean-label, vegan-certified format is the ideal product for this buyer.

**Creative instinct:** Highest-opportunity untapped segment in the pipeline. "Creatine for women" is the fastest-growing creatine content segment on TikTok in 2025. First-mover advantage is real, and the window is open now.
