## VOC Research Summary

### What Customers Actually Buy For

**1. Taste as permission** — The single most unprompted mention across all sources. Customers didn't buy Nektar for taste, but taste is what converts them to long-term subscribers. "First supplement I've actually stuck with" is the retention mechanism. Nektar's clearest competitive moat — and it lives almost entirely outside paid media.

**2. Organ-level fear, not wellness aspiration** — The emotional driver is not "I want to be healthier." It's "my dad had a heart attack at 58 and I don't know what I'm doing for mine." Customers are responding to fear of invisible, unnamed decline — not fitness optimization.

**3. Proof of mechanism, not proof of benefit** — VOC repeatedly asks "what does it actually do?" and "how do I know it's working?" Named organs + named ingredients is the answer — but it has to be taught, not just stated.

**4. Price is a permission structure** — "$42 for this quality is a steal" and "cheaper than AG1" appear in reviews, but price isn't the hook. It's the internal justification after they've decided to try it.

**5. Stack compatibility** — Multiple reviewers mix Nektar with Atlas creatine without flavor interference. Underutilized cross-sell and retention signal.

## ICP Avatars

### ICP 1 — "Marcus" (Primary · 35–55)

Works in sales, trades, or management. Family man. His dad had a heart attack at 58. His doctor recently said his cholesterol was "borderline" or blood pressure was 128/82. He's been trying things — fish oil, a multivitamin, maybe AG1 for two weeks before canceling. He doesn't say "I want to optimize my health." He says "I want to not end up like my dad."

**Why he's skeptical:** The last greens powder tasted like lawn clippings and he couldn't tell if it was doing anything. He's not afraid of $42 — he's afraid of wasting $42 on something that disappears into his cabinet.

**How to win him:** Name the organ he's worried about in the first 3 seconds. Give him a real ingredient and what it does (CoQ10 = mitochondrial fuel for the heart). Show someone who looks like him having a genuine taste reaction. Make $42 feel obvious.

**Creative instinct:** Specificity over aspiration. "Heart, liver, and kidneys" hits differently than "overall wellness."

## Key Strategic Observations

1. **IM8 is the new category threat.** ~450 active Meta ads, David Beckham, NASA's Chief Scientist, NSF Certified. Nektar cannot out-celebrity them. Counter-move: organ specificity (IM8 doesn't name organs) + taste (IM8 taste reviews mixed) + price (IM8 = $78, Nektar = $42). All three are defensible without a dollar in celebrity fees.
2. **Organifi and Supergreen Tonik going dark = warm buyer pool.** Their subscribers are actively shopping alternatives. A keyword conquest ad ("Tried Organifi? Here's why people are switching.") could capture high-intent lapsed buyers at low CAC.
3. **Prop 65 warning is a live conversion killer.** A 1★ Amazon review (Feb 2026) explicitly calls out the California Prop 65 lead warning. Zero creative response exists. A factual explainer would convert a skeptic segment currently bouncing silently.
4. **The medication audience is the most valuable underserved segment.** The TikTok comment with 26 likes asking about blood pressure medication signals a 45–60 demographic with family-history triggers. A single FAQ creative + dedicated landing page section would own this segment. No competitor addresses it.
5. **Seed is an adjacent competitor with a shared ICP and a stack opportunity.** 66 Meta ads, $50/month, gut-first, clinically proven. Their buyers are warm Nektar prospects. Stack angle: "Seed covers your gut. Nektar covers your organs." Conquest angle: same gut benefit, three more organs, $8 less.
6. **Taste is the unlock for every other message.** Until a buyer sees Nektar's taste proved on camera, all other claims face the "sounds like every other greens powder" objection. Taste proof is the foundation of the creative system — not a nice-to-have.
